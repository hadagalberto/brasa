The most expensive colors in any codebase are the ones nobody chose. They arrive as hex values copied from a slide deck three rebrands ago, accrete one `!important` at a time, and defend themselves with the sheer difficulty of knowing what they *do*. Our design system had accumulated a hundred of them. This is the story of trading them for eleven numbers and a color space that tells the truth.

## Why hex codes lie

Hex is a filing format for monitors, not a description of color. The lie is easiest to see in a palette's grays:

| Token | Hex | Perceptual lightness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| gray-100 | #f5f5f5 | very light, neutral |
| amber-100 | #fef3c7 | *similar* lightness, warm |
| brand-50 | #fff7ed | similar again, warmer still |

Three "light" colors, three different feelings — and in hex, the reason is invisible. OKLCH puts the answer in the string: `oklch(0.98 0.01 80)` *reads* as "98% light, barely colorful, warm yellow hue." Lightness, chroma, hue — the three axes a designer actually decides — become the three numbers in the file.

The practical difference is not aesthetic, it is *reasoning*. When a contrast question comes up, "this surface is L 0.98 and the ink is L 0.23" answers it in one step. In hex, the same question means opening a calculator and hoping the result survives the next theme.

## The eleven numbers

Brasa's entire dark theme is eleven perceptual decisions — the rest derives:

```css
:root {
  --canvas: oklch(0.16 0.012 40);      /* near-black, warm */
  --surface: oklch(0.19 0.014 42);     /* layered by lightness */
  --ink: oklch(0.94 0.008 85);          /* warm white, never #fff */
  --accent-hue: 35;                      /* the one that re-themes everything */
}
```

Notice what is *not* there: no hex codes at all, and one hue to rule them. Every ember step is computed relative to `--accent-hue` — 35 for the deep red-orange the brand ships with, and a single edit produces a coherent warm-gold or a cool ember-green without touching anything else:

<Callout variant="tip" title="Steal this rule">
One hue, many lightnesses. When every accent in the system shares a hue and varies only in L and C, "does this go together?" stops being a meeting — the answer is yes, and the only remaining question is *emphasis*, which lightness answers out loud.
</Callout>

## What OKLCH changed in practice

Three meetings changed shape:

**Contrast reviews became arithmetic.** WCAG contrast ratios need relative luminance, and OKLCH's lightness component maps to it directly enough to sanity-check by eye. "Dark theme body text" stopped being a sampling exercise and became "ink at L 0.94 on canvas at L 0.16 — comfortably AAA."

**Theme variants became a diff, not a project.** Light mode is the same eleven numbers retuned: canvas up to L 0.98 with the hue kept warm, ink down to L 0.23, accent *desaturated and darkened* so it holds contrast on paper. Twelve lines of CSS, not a second palette to defend.

**The accent became a product feature.** Because the scale is hue-relative, "re-theme the site to your brand" is one config value. We test it by setting the hue to values we would never ship — 130 renders a coherent sage green, 260 an eerie violet — and every time, *everything* follows: buttons, code blocks, focus rings, the OG images. Coherence is not talent; it is structure.

## The honest caveats

OKLCH is not magic, and two limitations keep us humble:

First, **chroma is unbounded** in the notation but capped by your display's gamut — `oklch(0.7 0.4 35)` describes a color most screens cannot show, and browsers silently clamp it to the nearest representable value. Keep chroma under ~0.21 for warm accents and the clamps stay invisible.

Second, **perceptually uniform is monitor-relative.** OKLCH is uniform in the Oklab model, not on any specific panel. It is dramatically better than hex for reasoning, and still no substitute for a contrast check before you ship body text. We keep both: the space for taste, the calculator for the floor.

The deeper change was never the color space, though. It is that the palette became *legible*. A new engineer can read eleven numbered decisions and understand the entire visual temperature of the product — and when someone asks for "a slightly warmer dark mode," the answer is now a number, not an archaeology project.
