The first thing this blog's build produces is not the HTML — it is `rss.xml` and `feed.json`, standing next to the homepage like a driveway and a mailbox. In 2026, shipping feeds is an unfashionable act of hospitality: you are telling readers they can subscribe to your work *without an algorithm deciding whether you exist today*.

We think that is worth ten minutes of build code. This post is the defense, plus the concrete how.

## Why two formats and not one

RSS is the survivor: fifteen years of "dead" and still the default subscribe format of every reader app on every platform. JSON Feed is the newcomer that fixed RSS's real problems — escaping, dates, enclosures — by being boring, well-specified JSON.

We ship both because they cost nearly nothing and serve different readers:

## How the build makes them

Both feeds are prerendered endpoints over the same content collection the blog pages read — one source of truth, three projections (HTML pages, XML, JSON). Nothing is generated "at publish time" by a separate tool, so a feed item can never drift from the article it points at.

The RSS endpoint builds its XML by hand rather than through a library, which sounds gratuitous until you have debugged a dependency's CDATA handling at midnight. Total cost: ~40 lines and an escape function.

<Callout variant="note" title="The three validator bugs everyone hits first">
CDATA sections that assume angle brackets away; <code>pubDate</code>s formatted by anything other than RFC 822 (readers silently drop the item); and a missing <code>guid</code>, which teaches every reader app that your posts are new again. Our build test asserts all three, in both formats.
</Callout>

## The subscribe page as a feature

The last piece is social: feeds die quietly when the URL is a secret. This blog links its feeds from the footer, from the blog header, and from every article's share row — and the link itself is a small act of self-respect, a statement that the writing is worth following on the reader's terms.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Adopt a reader this week">
If you have never lived inside a feed reader, the argument is abstract. Try fourteen days of one: a slow morning, a coffee, forty subscriptions you chose, no engagement ranking, nothing infinite. Most people who do this end up shipping feeds at work — the format sells itself the moment you are the reader.
</Callout>

The web's original promise was that *you* choose what you follow. Feeds are that promise, still working, still boring, still free. Ship them.
