Nobody reads on the web; everybody skims until something catches, then reads properly. The honest blog design serves both phases of that behavior: a *scannable* surface — index, tags, minutes — and a *comfortable* reading room once someone commits. Most blog themes bolt on plugins for both. We treated them as design problems, which mostly means asking one question per feature: *what promise does this make to the reader?*

## Reading time: an honest estimate, stated gently

The "8 min read" chip makes a specific promise: *committing to this costs about a coffee*. Get it wrong and the trust leaks — a "4 min read" that eats twenty minutes is a small, stupid betrayal that readers remember.

So the math stays honest and the presentation stays humble:

- **Words in, minutes out.** A plain word count over the readable text (code blocks and component markup excluded — nobody reads a code fence at 200 wpm), at 200 words per minute, rounded up, floored at one minute. No per-section weighted voodoo; we tested variants against actual reading time on our own team and the simple version won.
- **Stated as "~", rendered as "8 min read".** A range cosplaying as a measurement is the fastest way to make the number meaningless.

<Callout variant="tip" title="The number is for the skimmer's calendar">
Reading time is not about speed — it is about *decision support*. "Do I read this now, save it for lunch, or never?" is the reader's actual question at the index, and a number answers it more kindly than a wall of paragraphs does.
</Callout>

## The TOC: a map that knows where you are

A table of contents on a long essay is a map; the craft is keeping the "you are here" pin honest. Ours is sticky on wide viewports, a plain collapsible list on narrow ones, and highlights the current section via an IntersectionObserver that watches headings — the same markup, degraded gracefully.

Three small decisions do most of the work:

1. **Two levels, no more.** h2 and h3; deeper nesting is a *writing* smell the TOC should not amortize.
2. **Highlight, don't scroll-hijack.** The active entry gets color and an ember marker; the panel never auto-scrolls itself. The map stays still while the territory moves.
3. **Anchors that survive anything.** Heading IDs are stable slugs, so a shared deep link still lands on the argument, not the top of the page — a promise to the person who sent the link as much as to the one who receives it.

## Related posts: the promise of "more like this"

"Related posts" is the cheapest feature to fake and the easiest to make meaningless. Sorting by recency and calling it related breaks the promise in the reader's presence — they clicked because of *this* topic.

Ours scores by shared tags, ties broken by date, capped at two. Two, because the end of an essay is a doorway, not a shopping aisle: one strong follow-on invites, three starts to look like a retention strategy. And the same machinery quietly powers the tags index and the author pages — one notion of *affinity*, expressed wherever a reader might want it.

## Pagination: the archive you can hold

Infinite scroll is a promise nobody should make to a scholar: that there is no *where* in your archive. Numbered pages are coordinates — citable, bookmarkable, cacheable — and the page count printed next to them tells the truth about how much writing lives here. Our index paginates at six posts with a featured essay above page one, so the front door is a curated issue and the pages behind it are the honest ledger.

## The reading room itself

Once a reader commits, the page mostly needs to *get out of the way*, which is the hardest kind of design to take credit for:

| Decision | Rationale |
| --- | --- |
| Max measure ~62ch | two to three alphabets per line; the eye returns to the left edge without hunting |
| Fluid type, one step larger than UI text | body `clamp()` up to 19px — long-form deserves the bump |
| `text-wrap: balance` headings, `pretty` paragraphs | ragged-right without orphaned words, free from the platform |
| Warm off-black text on warm paper (dark or light) | the "off" is contrast *and* temperature — see the contrast post |
| No mid-article interstitials | the essay's one promise is uninterrupted flow |

> A good essay page is furniture you stop noticing, in the specific way you stop noticing a chair that fits.

The through-line of all of it: every one of these features is a *promise with a number or a link attached* — minutes, sections, coordinates, follow-ons. The web is full of blogs that treat readers as traffic; the craft is treating them as readers, with all the gentle machinery that implies.
